Lecture 42

Gauss and Stokes

What this lecture does

Boundary laws compare an integral of a derivative over a region with an integral of the original field on its boundary. We prove the divergence theorem for a box and give its compact-solid extension as a sketch. We state the general Stokes theorem with its orientation convention and prove the identity on one admissible parameter patch. Explicit sphere and hemisphere calculations test the hypotheses and signs, including the effect of a singularity inside a solid. A stated conservation model then connects local density changes to boundary flux.

42.2 The compact-solid extension and internal boundaries

42.4 Stokes’s theorem and the induced boundary direction

An oriented surface has a chosen normal; its boundary direction must be chosen compatibly. Locally, traversing the boundary with the surface on the left and the chosen normal pointing upwards describes the convention. Equivalently, the right-hand rule relates the positive normal to the positive boundary direction. For an upward planar disc this direction is anticlockwise as seen from above. For several boundary components, this rule applies to each component with the same chosen surface orientation.

Theorem 42.2 (Stokes’s theorem, cited). Let SSS be a compact oriented piecewise C2C^2C2 surface with piecewise C1C^1C1 boundary, with finitely many regular parametrised pieces and compatible normals. Let F\vv FF be C1C^1C1 on an open neighbourhood of SSS. Orient every component of ∂S\partial S∂S by the induced boundary convention. Then ∮∂SF⋅dx=∬Scurl⁡F⋅n dS.\oint_{\partial S}\vv F\cdot\dd\vv x =\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vv F\cdot\vv n\,\dd S.∮∂S​F⋅dx=∬S​curlF⋅ndS.

The general assertion is cited [C]: Stewart, §16.8. For finitely many boundary components, including an empty boundary, an accessible primary statement is J. Stanfill, Calculus III Lecture Notes, Baylor, §30, Theorem 30.1 and its following closed-surface note, printed p. 147. Its preceding paragraph permits finite unions of patches and boundary exceptions. Our piecewise C2C^2C2 and neighbourhood C1C^1C1 hypotheses are sufficient for that statement. The single-boundary-curve form is also B. Poonen, MIT 18.02 notes, Fall 2021, Theorem 19.1, printed p. 133. No smoothness throughout a solid enclosed by SSS is requested by Stokes; the field must be C1C^1C1 near the whole surface itself. A singular point on that surface would invalidate the hypothesis.

42.7 Consequences and a conservation model

The boundary laws also explain when one integration surface may be replaced by another. Suppose two surfaces satisfy Stokes’s hypotheses for the same field, have the same boundary curves, and induce the same direction on every boundary component. Applying the cited theorem to each gives ∬S1curl⁡F⋅n1 dS=∮CF⋅dx=∬S2curl⁡F⋅n2 dS.\iint_{S_1}\operatorname{curl}\vv F\cdot\vv n_1\,\dd S =\oint_C\vv F\cdot\dd\vv x =\iint_{S_2}\operatorname{curl}\vv F\cdot\vv n_2\,\dd S.∬S1​​curlF⋅n1​dS=∮C​F⋅dx=∬S2​​curlF⋅n2​dS. This surface-replacement conclusion inherits [C]. The field must be C1C^1C1 near both surfaces; it is not enough to check only the common boundary. No region between the surfaces is needed for this argument. For a closed surface the boundary is empty, so Stokes gives zero total flux of a curl [C]. This applies only when that curl comes from a field with the specified neighbourhood regularity. A field that merely has zero divergence on a punctured domain need not be such a curl near a whole surrounding sphere, as Sheet 42 asks you to show.

There is a corresponding scalar consequence of Gauss. If vvv is C2C^2C2 near a box or a solid of the stated Gauss class, then ∇v\nabla v∇v is C1C^1C1 there. Its normal component is called the outward normal derivative, ∂nv=∇v⋅n\partial_n v=\nabla v\cdot\vv n∂n​v=∇v⋅n. Gauss gives ∬∂K∂nv dS=∭KΔv dV.\iint_{\partial K}\partial_n v\,\dd S=\iiint_K\Delta v\,\dd V.∬∂K​∂n​vdS=∭K​ΔvdV. The formula is [P] for a box and [S] for the general solid class, exactly like Gauss. In particular, a harmonic function has zero total outward normal derivative under those hypotheses. This is a statement about the sum of the boundary contributions, not a pointwise claim that its normal derivative vanishes everywhere.

For a physical application, let ρ(t,x)≥0\rho(t,x)\geq0ρ(t,x)≥0 be mass per unit volume and let J(t,x)\vv J(t,x)J(t,x) be mass flux per unit area per unit time. Assume both are C1C^1C1 jointly on an open time–space domain. Fix a time and a spatial box whose neighbourhood stays in that domain for a small time interval. The model of mass conservation on this fixed box is the assumption ddt∭Kρ(t,x) dV=−∬∂KJ(t,x)⋅n dS.\begin{equation} \label{eq:42:balance} \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\iiint_K\rho(t,x)\,\dd V =-\iint_{\partial K}\vv J(t,x)\cdot\vv n\,\dd S. \end{equation}dtd​∭K​ρ(t,x)dV=−∬∂K​J(t,x)⋅ndS.(42.2) Positive outward flux removes mass from the box, which fixes the minus sign. This is a physical premise, not a mathematical theorem about every pair of functions ρ,J\rho,\vv Jρ,J.

The parameter clause of M applies on compact time subintervals and this fixed compact box because of joint C1C^1C1 regularity on a neighbourhood. It moves the time derivative inside the proper integral. The proved box divergence theorem then turns the assumed balance into ∭K(∂tρ+div⁡xJ) dV=0.\iiint_K\bigl(\partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}_x\vv J\bigr)\,\dd V=0.∭K​(∂t​ρ+divx​J)dV=0. The subscript xxx means divergence in the three spatial coordinates. If the model balance holds for every sufficiently small spatial box, this integral identity implies the pointwise continuity equation ∂tρ+div⁡xJ=0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:42:continuity} \partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}_x\vv J=0. \end{equation}∂t​ρ+divx​J=0.(42.3) Here is the full localisation argument. At a fixed time the integrand is continuous. If its value at one point were positive, continuity would make it at least half that positive value on a sufficiently small box around the point. Its integral would be positive, contradicting the identity. A negative value gives the same contradiction with reversed sign. Hence it vanishes at every point. Conversely, integrate the pointwise equation on any such box, apply M’s parameter rule and the box divergence theorem in reverse order, and recover the model balance. Thus the equivalence is proved [P from M] under the stated smoothness and all-small-box assumption.

Summary

  • Gauss equates total outward boundary flux to the volume integral of divergence for a box [P]. The stated compact-solid finite-graph extension is [S]; its field must be C1C^1C1 near the entire solid.
  • Every boundary component is outward from the solid. On a shell this means a radially inward normal on the inner sphere. An inverse-square singularity prevents using Gauss on a ball containing it when κ≠0\kappa\ne0κ=0.
  • General Stokes [C] requires the stated compact oriented surface, compatible boundary orientation and a field C1C^1C1 near the surface. Its single admissible C2C^2C2 patch case follows from Green [P].
  • Under the stated joint C1C^1C1 hypotheses, mass balance on every small box is equivalent to the continuity equation Equation (42.3) [P from M], with the physical balance law retained as an assumption.
  • The disc and upper hemisphere calculations both give π\piπ for the stated field and common induced circle direction. These are direct checks, rather than a proof of the general cited theorem.

Sheet 42 applies the boundary laws while checking normals, holes, singularities and the dependence of each route on proved, sketched or cited inputs. This completes the course’s passage from limits and local linear approximations to integral relations over regions and boundaries.

4 At a fixed time, a continuous function g=∂tρ+div⁡xJg=\partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}_x\mathbf Jg=∂t​ρ+divx​J has integral zero over one particular box. Choose the conclusion that follows from this single fact.

Here is the hint — try once more. Positive and negative contributions can cancel in one fixed region. One box integral can vanish by cancellation. The lecture's pointwise conclusion uses zero integrals over all sufficiently small boxes: continuity would make a nonzero value keep its sign on one such box, contradicting its zero integral. The physical balance law is an assumption of that argument.
