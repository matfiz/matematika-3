Lecture 29

Numerical and signed series

An infinite sum is a limit of finite partial sums. We first establish convergence tests for nonnegative terms, then handle signs by absolute convergence and alternating estimates. The tests also prove two growth limits needed for power and Taylor series.

Uses.

29.1 Partial sums define the infinite sum

Definition 29.1. For a real sequence (an)n≥1(a_n)_{n\geq1}(an​)n≥1​, its partial sums are SN=∑n=1NanS_N=\sum_{n=1}^N a_nSN​=∑n=1N​an​. The series ∑n=1∞an\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n∑n=1∞​an​ converges to S∈RS\in\RS∈R if SN→SS_N\to SSN​→S. Otherwise it diverges. The number SSS, when it exists, is its sum.

Adding or removing finitely many initial terms changes partial sums by a fixed finite amount once the cutoff passes those terms. Thus it changes the sum accordingly, without changing convergence. Series starting at index 0 use the same definition with that initial index. Linearity follows from finite-sum linearity and the limit laws: if two series converge, their termwise linear combination converges to the same linear combination of their sums.

Convergence forces an→0a_n\to0an​→0. Indeed, an=Sn−Sn−1a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}an​=Sn​−Sn−1​ for n≥2n\geq2n≥2, and both partial-sum sequences tend to SSS, so their difference tends to zero. This term test disproves convergence when terms do not tend to zero. A zero term limit by itself proves nothing about the sum.

29.2 A criterion in terms of finite tails

Convergence can be tested without knowing the sum beforehand. The next criterion says that every sufficiently late finite tail must be small, regardless of how many terms that tail contains.

Theorem 29.3 (Cauchy criterion for a series). A real series converges if and only if, for every η>0\eta>0η>0, some integer NNN satisfies ∣∑k=n+1mak∣<ηfor all integers m>n≥N.\left|\sum_{k=n+1}^m a_k\right|<\eta \qquad\text{for all integers }m>n\geq N.​k=n+1∑m​ak​​<ηfor all integers m>n≥N.

Proof. If Sn→SS_n\to SSn​→S, choose NNN such that ∣Sn−S∣<η/2|S_n-S|<\eta/2∣Sn​−S∣<η/2 for n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N. The tail is Sm−SnS_m-S_nSm​−Sn​, whose absolute value is less than η\etaη by the triangle inequality. This proves necessity.

Conversely, assume the tail condition. With tolerance 1, it bounds all late partial sums within 1 of one fixed partial sum. The finitely many earlier sums are bounded as well, so (Sn)(S_n)(Sn​) is bounded. Bolzano–Weierstrass from Lecture 4 gives a subsequence Snj→SS_{n_j}\to SSnj​​→S. For a new tolerance η>0\eta>0η>0, apply the tail condition with η/2\eta/2η/2 and choose one subsequence index nj≥Nn_j\geq Nnj​≥N with ∣Snj−S∣<η/2|S_{n_j}-S|<\eta/2∣Snj​​−S∣<η/2. The term test concerns a single late term. This criterion controls an entire late block, with no fixed bound on the block’s length. Harmonic terms tend to zero, but their dyadic blocks in the preceding example are at least 1/21/21/2, violating the criterion.

29.5 The root and integral tests

Choosing a test.

First check whether the terms tend to zero: if not, the series diverges. For nonnegative terms, look for a known geometric or power comparison; factorials and repeated powers often make the ratio or root test convenient. If their limit is 1, that test is inconclusive, not a convergence verdict: both ∑1/n\sum1/n∑1/n and ∑1/n2\sum1/n^2∑1/n2 have ratio and root limits 1, but only the second converges. A positive decreasing function may invite the integral test. For signed terms, first ask whether the absolute series converges; otherwise the alternating test below can sometimes settle convergence and give an error bound. These are choices to try, not a mandatory algorithm.

29.6 Alternating sums and their error

Theorem 29.14 (Alternating-series test). If bn≥0b_n\geq0bn​≥0, bn+1≤bnb_{n+1}\leq b_nbn+1​≤bn​ and bn→0b_n\to0bn​→0, then ∑n=1∞(−1)n−1bn\sum_{n=1}^\infty(-1)^{n-1}b_n∑n=1∞​(−1)n−1bn​ converges to a number SSS. The alternating harmonic series ∑n=1∞(−1)n−1/n\sum_{n=1}^\infty(-1)^{n-1}/n∑n=1∞​(−1)n−1/n converges by this theorem, but its absolute-value series is the divergent harmonic series. Grouping consecutive terms is a separate operation from changing their order. Let 0=N0<N1<N2<⋯0=N_0<N_1<N_2<\cdots0=N0​<N1​<N2​<⋯ be integers, and replace each finite block by cj=∑n=Nj−1+1Njanc_j=\sum_{n=N_{j-1}+1}^{N_j}a_ncj​=∑n=Nj−1​+1Nj​​an​. The grouped partial sums are exactly SNjS_{N_j}SNj​​. Since Nj≥jN_j\geq jNj​≥j, they form a subsequence tending to the original sum whenever the original series converges. Thus such grouping preserves an already established sum.

The converse need not hold. Grouping 1−1+1−1+⋯1-1+1-1+\cdots1−1+1−1+⋯ into adjacent pairs gives a series of zeros, but the original partial sums alternate between 1 and 0. The grouped sums have discarded those odd cutoffs. Consequently a convergent grouped series alone does not establish convergence of its ungrouped source. This distinction will matter when separating terms of Taylor series.

A rearrangement of a series replaces its order by a bijection σ:N→N\sigma:\N\to\Nσ:N→N, producing ∑n=1∞aσ(n)\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{\sigma(n)}∑n=1∞​aσ(n)​. For conditionally convergent real series, such a change can alter the sum. Sheet 29 proves this warning and the stronger statement that any prescribed real sum can be obtained. The rearrangement result does not license arbitrary regrouping before convergence has been justified.

Summary

  • Series convergence means convergence of partial sums. It forces an→0a_n\to0an​→0; the harmonic series shows the converse is false.
  • Theorem 29.3: convergence is equivalent to every sufficiently late finite tail being small, uniformly over both tail endpoints.
  • Nonnegative series converge exactly when their partial sums are bounded. Comparison and positive finite limit comparison transfer convergence.
  • Theorems 29.7, 29.8 and 29.11: absolute convergence implies convergence. Ratio and root limits below 1 give absolute convergence, above 1 give divergence; at 1 they are inconclusive. The root proof is on Sheet 29.
  • Theorem 29.12: nonnegative nonincreasing functions give an integral test with two-sided tail bounds. The series ∑n−p\sum n^{-p}∑n−p converges exactly for p>1p>1p>1.
  • Theorem 29.14: nonincreasing nonnegative terms tending to zero give an alternating sum with error bounded by the first omitted term.
  • An eventual geometric bound on consecutive absolute terms gives the quantitative remainder estimate Equation (29.2), starting with the first omitted term.
  • The limits in Equation (29.1) hold for every real ccc and ∣q∣<1|q|<1∣q∣<1. Conditional rearrangements can change a sum; the proof is on Sheet 29.
