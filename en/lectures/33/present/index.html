Lecture 33

First-order differential equations

A differential equation prescribes a derivative using time and the unknown quantity itself. An initial value selects a solution, when one exists uniquely. Separation and an integrating factor construct solutions for two classes of equations. Their proofs also identify the intervals on which the formulas solve the original problem.

Uses.

  • Summary 5: Continuity and one-sided limits are defined by error bounds.

  • Summary 6: A continuous scalar function on an interval takes every intermediate value.

  • Summary 8: Product, chain, inverse and elementary derivative rules hold on their stated domains.

  • Summary 9: A derivative with strict constant sign gives strict monotonicity; a zero derivative gives a constant on an interval.

  • Summary 12: Euclidean distance and the norm measure errors in the plane.

  • Summary 16: Complex functions of a real parameter differentiate componentwise. The exponential never vanishes and (eλt)′=λeλt(e^{\lambda t})'=\lambda e^{\lambda t}(eλt)′=λeλt for λ∈C\lambda\in\Cλ∈C.

  • Summary 23: Primitives on an interval differ by a constant. Substitution and integration by parts evaluate primitives.

  • Summary 26: The fundamental theorem differentiates the integral of a continuous function and evaluates definite integrals by primitives.

33.1 An equation and its initial value

An ordinary differential equation relates a function of one real variable to its derivatives. Its order is the highest derivative that occurs. A scalar first-order equation in explicit form is y′(t)=F(t,y(t)).y'(t)=F(t,y(t)).y′(t)=F(t,y(t)). The function FFF is given. The real-valued function yyy is unknown. A solution on an open interval III is a differentiable map y:I→Ry:I\to\Ry:I→R whose graph lies in the domain of FFF and which satisfies the equation at every t∈It\in It∈I. An initial-value problem also prescribes y(t0)=y0y(t_0)=y_0y(t0​)=y0​, where t0∈It_0\in It0​∈I. The interval belongs to the answer. A formula that becomes undefined at one time cannot be a solution on an interval containing that time.

The value F(t,y)F(t,y)F(t,y) specifies the slope of a solution through the point (t,y)(t,y)(t,y) in the time–value plane. A direction field draws short segments with those slopes. The segments record the equation rather than an approximation theorem. A proposed curve still needs differentiation and substitution to establish that it is a solution.

For example, y′=yy'=yy′=y has solutions y(t)=Cety(t)=Ce^ty(t)=Cet for every C∈RC\in\RC∈R: differentiation gives y′=Cet=yy'=Ce^t=yy′=Cet=y. The initial condition y(0)=3y(0)=3y(0)=3 forces C=3C=3C=3 within this family. That calculation alone does not show that the family contains every solution. A uniqueness argument is needed for that conclusion.

33.2 Local existence and its hypotheses

Continuity of FFF near a point of the plane means that its value changes by a small amount whenever both coordinates change by a sufficiently small amount. The same LLL must work for all those times and pairs.

Summary

  • A solution satisfies its equation at every point of its stated interval. Theorem 33.1 gives local existence and uniqueness under continuity and a uniform local Lipschitz bound in the unknown value [C]. It does not assert global existence.
  • Theorem 33.4 justifies separation on value intervals where BBB is nonzero. Equilibria B(η)=0B(\eta)=0B(η)=0 are checked before division.
  • For continuous ppp and qqq on an open interval, the positive factor e∫t0tpe^{\int_{t_0}^t p}e∫t0​t​p gives the unique linear solution Equation (33.1) on that interval, for every real initial value.
  • Theorem 33.8 proves z(t)=z0eλ(t−t0)z(t)=z_0e^{\lambda(t-t_0)}z(t)=z0​eλ(t−t0​) uniquely for complex constants and real time. Its proof uses the zero-derivative theorem componentwise.
