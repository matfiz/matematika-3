Lecture 39

Triple integrals and moments

What this lecture does

A solid has a mass distribution over volume rather than area. Triple integrals evaluate its mass and moments; cylindrical and spherical coordinates make symmetry useful without losing the volume factor. We derive the moment of inertia of a uniform ball and then use ordinary radial limits to normalise a Gaussian distribution in three dimensions.

39.1 Volume integrals on compact solids

A small-volume mass sum has the same form as the small-area sum of Lecture 38, with cell volume in place of area. The triple Riemann integral is its common limit as the largest cell diameter tends to zero, independently of the selected sample points: ∭Kf dV=lim⁡∑jf(ξj)volume⁡(Kj).\iiint_K f\,\dd V=\lim\sum_j f(\xi_j)\operatorname{volume}(K_j).∭K​fdV=limj∑​f(ξj​)volume(Kj​). In particular, integrating 111 gives volume. Finite linearity, order and additivity follow from the corresponding properties of the finite sums. For nonnegative fff, inclusion of compact regions preserves integral order when the closure of their difference is a finite union of admissible graph solids, by additivity and positivity as in the plane.

Here a compact graph solid has a compact planar graph region DDD and bounds K={(x,y,z):(x,y)∈D, g1(x,y)≤z≤g2(x,y)},K=\{(x,y,z):(x,y)\in D,\ g_1(x,y)\leq z\leq g_2(x,y)\},K={(x,y,z):(x,y)∈D, g1​(x,y)≤z≤g2​(x,y)}, where g1≤g2g_1\leq g_2g1​≤g2​ are continuous. Coordinate permutations and finite unions of such pieces are allowed. The boundary is piecewise C1C^1C1 in this sense: it is covered by finitely many C1C^1C1 parametrised surface pieces, with parametrisations C1C^1C1 up to their parameter boundaries. Away from joining curves and finitely many joining points, each piece is locally a C1C^1C1 graph of one coordinate as a function of the other two. Graph regularity may fail at those joining locations, allowing, for example, a cone vertex. All regions used here are closed and bounded, and their boundaries have this property.
